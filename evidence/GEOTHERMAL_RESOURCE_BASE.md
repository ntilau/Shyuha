# Rwanda's Geothermal Resource Base

## Overview

This document establishes what is actually known about Rwanda's geothermal resource, what is inferred, and what remains entirely unknown. It supersedes the earlier `SUMMARY.md` and the resource sections of `HOT_SPRINGS_ENERGY_PROFITABILITY.md`, both of which contained factual errors corrected here.

The headline finding is uncomfortable but essential: **Rwanda has drilled three geothermal exploration wells and all three were unsuccessful.** The remaining prospect of interest is Gisenyi, where geothermometry suggests a deeper resource but no well has tested it. The national potential estimate has fallen from "over 700 MW" to a maximum of about 90 MWe as exploration has proceeded.

## What Is Actually Measured

Only **surface spring temperatures** have been measured. There are no measured flow rates at any Rwandan geothermal spring, and no measured reservoir temperature below ground.

| Location | Measured surface temperature | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gisenyi (Rubavu), west of peninsula | **73.6 °C**, EC 3,100 µS/cm | JICA site visit, Jan 2013 | High |
| Gisenyi, east of peninsula | **70.3 °C**, EC 2,700 µS/cm | JICA site visit, Jan 2013 | High |
| Gisenyi | **70–75 °C** | Chevron 2006 sampling | High |
| Gisenyi | ~**75 °C** | WGC 2020 country update | High |
| Karago (near Karisimbi) | **72.5 °C**, EC 1,100–1,200 µS/cm | JICA site visit, Jan 2013 | High |
| Karago | **64 °C** | WGC 2020 country update | High |
| Mashyuza (Rusizi) | **42–54 °C** | Chevron 2006 sampling | High |
| Bugarama | ~**55 °C** | WGC 2020 country update | High |
| Kinigi | **No hot springs reported** | JICA prospect description | High |

The Gisenyi figures differ because they are different springs sampled on different dates. **Cite the specific spring and year, not a single number.**

**The frequently quoted "70–75 °C" is a surface spring discharge temperature.** It is not a reservoir temperature and must not be used as one. Earlier repository analysis used it as "Resource Temperature: 70-75°C+ (suitable for binary cycle power plants)", which conflates the two and understates the uncertainty in the power case.

## What Is Inferred, Not Measured

Reservoir temperatures below ground have been **estimated by geothermometry**, not measured by drilling:

- **BGR gas geothermometry (C1/C2)**: Ntango **342 °C**, Mubona **232 °C**, Karago **226 °C**, Gisenyi **209 °C**.
- **BGR general finding**: geothermometers applied to BRGM (1982) and Chevron (2006) sample data indicate reservoir temperatures of **150–210 °C**.
- **JICA interpretation**: the system is of medium temperature, **100–200 °C**, "ideal for binary cycle electricity generation and direct uses."
- **Geo2D / GDC study at Gisenyi (findings presented June 2017, EU-funded)**: a shallow reservoir at **~100 °C** and a deeper reservoir at **~160 °C**, with the deepest part at **~200 °C**. Recommended magnetotelluric surveys and **slim-hole drilling** across the fault and peninsula.

Geophysics is also inferential. KenGen ran **43 magnetotelluric and 36 transient electromagnetic soundings** (Jan–Oct 2008). A low-resistivity zone southwest of the Karisimbi stratovolcano matched the geochemical results, and a conductive body — inferred intruded magma in granitic basement — was interpreted at roughly **5 km depth**.

**None of these estimates has been confirmed by a well.**

## Prospect-by-Prospect Assessment

### Gisenyi — the remaining priority

**Status: undrilled at depth; the only prospect with a live case.**

The Geo2D/GDC study identified four areas of interest: **Kilwa (Area 1), Gisenyi (Area 2), Muti (Area 3) and Kanzanza (Area 4)**. Geochemistry from JICA's own analysis concluded that "**Gisenyi and Karago are probably the most promising locations**," and Chevron (2006) concluded Gisenyi was the most promising prospect.

As of **October 2024**, the government had contributed **~$200,000 of the ~$800,000 required** for exploration at Gisenyi, was **drilling shallow wells** to understand the reservoir, and was seeking funding to drill deeper. REG's stated near-term focus was **direct heat utilisation**, with a pilot combining geothermal and solar for a cold storage system. Negotiations with GRMF were ongoing, with Rwanda qualified but required to co-contribute.

**Grid advantage.** The Gisenyi hot springs are on the Lake Kivu shore within Rubavu town — the same locality as the **Rubavu 220 kV substation**, which is reported complete and ready for commissioning (93% complete in the FY2024-25 infrastructure report, with civil works at 99%). No published point-to-point distance exists, but the co-location is materially favourable. **A network study is still required before any interconnection figure is quoted.**

### Karisimbi — drilled and conclusively failed

**Status: closed. Should not be revisited without new evidence.**

This corrects the earlier repository claim of "**2006**: Two deep wells drilled at Karisimbi (no viable system found)." The drilling actually took place in **2013–2014**, and it was more decisive than the earlier text implied:

- **KW01** drilled to **3,015 m** (from July 2013)
- **KW03** drilled to **1,367 m** (from December 2013)
- Drilling **terminated March 2014**

The result, per the WGC 2020 country update: "**there was no evidence of a geothermal system in the Karisimbi area**." The reason is stated precisely: "Alteration mineralogy and measured temperatures in the two wells are consistent with **normal continental geothermal gradient (i.e. ~30 °C/km) conclusively demonstrating that there is not a geothermal reservoir under the southern slopes of Karisimbi**."

Three wells were planned and government-funded; only two were drilled. Contractor was Great Wall Drilling Company (China), well testing by GDC Kenya. Total national resource potential attributed to Karisimbi in the 2011 assessment had been **320 MWe** — the largest single prospect in the country.

**Note the repository's later text still listed Karisimbi as a "planned test site for future development," which contradicts this result.** That inconsistency is resolved here: Karisimbi is closed.

### Kinigi — ruled out

**Status: closed on current evidence.**

Detailed surface studies and an ESIA were completed. Supplementary surveys were run in June 2015 by West Japan Engineering Consultants (WestJec), funded by JICA. At a Technical Review Meeting in **May 2016**, "it was agreed that there is **no conclusive sign of geothermal system in the Kinigi study area**."

Rwanda's GRMF Expression of Interest for slim-hole drilling at Kinigi had been **qualified for grant award in 2015, but the award procedure was suspended** following that review. Whether the suspension was ever lifted is **unverified**.

Note that BTC had scheduled **EUR 27 million** for Kinigi exploratory drilling, to be confirmed in early 2014 — that programme did not proceed to drilling.

### Bugarama — low enthalpy, direct use only

**Status: not a power prospect; possible direct-use site.**

An EU-funded regional study (DRC/Burundi/Rwanda) under the Great Lakes Energy Agency, with Reykjavik Geothermal as consultant, began **November 2013**. Surface exploration and **thermal gradient wells** were completed — Bugarama is the only prospect where gradient wells are recorded.

Conclusion: "**Bugarama is a typical low enthalpy system and no localized heat source is present**." Predicted subsurface temperatures from silica geothermometers are **75–115 °C**, with depth to resource **≥ 1,100 m**. The study recommended additional surveys and a **direct-use market study**.

At ~55 °C surface temperature, Bugarama is a heat prospect at best.

### Mashyuza (Rusizi)

Sampled at **42–54 °C**. A news report from September 2020 described the spring as "still flowing at **15–20 litres per second**" during a drainage incident. This is **journalism, not a survey measurement**, and should be treated as indicative only. Note also that JICA explicitly states Mashyuza is **not** part of the Karisimbi prospect.

## National Potential Estimates — Reconciled

This is the reconciliation the earlier repository documents lacked. The estimates in circulation span a factor of eight, and they descend as exploration proceeds:

| Estimate | Source | Date | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| 170–300 MW | Geothermal Association / GEA | 1999 | Desktop |
| **740 MWe** | MININFRA assessment (Table 4-7 in JICA report) | Dec 2011 | Per-prospect desktop estimate |
| "over 700 MWe"; "300 MWe in Karisimbi" | JICA report summary | 2013 | Same as above |
| **Maximum ~90 MWe** | JICA Electricity Master Plan work | 2015 | Post-drilling revision |
| Up to 100 MW | Rwanda Energy Group / 2015 Master Plan | — | Commonly quoted |
| >20 MW short-term, >300 MW long-term | MININFRA (earlier repository summary) | — | Unreconciled |

The 2011 MININFRA breakdown was: **Karisimbi 320 MWe, Gisenyi 200 MWe, Kinigi 120 MWe, Bugarama 60 MWe, other 40 MWe.**

Critically, MININFRA's own 2011 assessment was explicitly qualified in the JICA report: "**This is only an estimate that needs to be confirmed by deep exploration drilling of at least three exploration wells in each prospect.**"

Since then, Karisimbi has been drilled and failed (320 MWe written off), Kinigi has been ruled out (120 MWe written off), and Bugarama has been characterised as low-enthalpy (60 MWe written off). That leaves **Gisenyi's 200 MWe desktop figure as essentially the entire remaining case** — and it too is unconfirmed, with the current national estimate capped at **~90 MWe**.

**This plan does not rely on any of these figures.** They are reported here for completeness and to show why the plan's resource assumptions are built on scenario ranges rather than a headline number.

## The Flow Rate Gap

**No measured geothermal flow rate exists publicly for Gisenyi, Karago/Karisimbi, or Kinigi.** This is the single largest data gap in the plan, because without a flow rate the size of any system — thermal or electrical — cannot be determined.

One figure is frequently misread as a flow measurement and is not:

**"~700 tonnes/hour for 10.5 MW"** appears in the JICA report as an **analogy, not a measurement**. JICA presents the Pico-Vermelho plant (São Miguel, Azores) — steam 151 °C, steam flow 74.86 t/h, brine flow 346.74 t/h, net 10.5 MW — then states that "the geothermal fluid of Karisimbi may be of 60% enthalpy of this example, and therefore **a simplified calculation indicates that approximate 700 t/h (195 kg/s) of geothermal fluid from wells in Karisimbi may be required** to produce a similar amount of power."

It is a back-of-envelope sizing assumption for a hypothetical plant at a prospect that has since been proven to have no reservoir. **It must never be presented as a measured Rwandan resource figure.**

A second commonly confused figure: **120 m³/hour** is the design capacity of the **drilling water supply** drawn from Karago Lake (18 km pipeline, 430 m head difference) — a drilling constraint, not a reservoir or plant parameter. JICA itself used it only as a scale comparison, noting the required power-plant fluid would be "six times as large as the planned water volume."

## Implications for This Plan

1. **The plan is a Gisenyi plan.** Karisimbi, Kinigi and Bugarama are closed for power generation on current evidence. Any plan that treats them as live prospects is not credible to a reader who knows the drilling history.
2. **The power case requires new information, not better modelling.** Only slim-hole drilling can determine whether the inferred 160–200 °C reservoir at Gisenyi exists. No financial model can substitute for that.
3. **Direct use is supportable now.** At measured 70–75 °C surface temperatures, greenhouse, aquaculture and low-temperature process heat are technically viable without drilling. Bugarama's low-enthalpy character makes it a direct-use site, not a write-off.
4. **The grid advantage at Gisenyi is real but unquantified.** The Rubavu 220 kV substation is co-located with the springs and near commissioning. This materially improves the power case — but no distance or interconnection cost figure should be quoted without a network study.
5. **The desirability of the venture's role is unchanged.** Rwanda has spent national money drilling three wells with no commercial result. The gap is not exploration appetite — it is a commercial vehicle that can convert a proven resource into revenue. That is what this plan proposes (see `../GOVERNANCE_AND_OWNERSHIP.md`).
