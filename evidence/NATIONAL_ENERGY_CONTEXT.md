# Rwanda's National Energy Context

## Overview

This document replaces the earlier `MININFRA.md` and the demand sections of the superseded repository analysis, both of which carried figures that were accurate when written and are now several years out of date. It establishes the market need that the venture addresses, using the most recent primary sources available.

The most important point for this plan: **Rwanda's remaining thermal generation is its most expensive power by a wide margin, and the country's own least-cost plan does not model geothermal capacity arriving before the mid-2030s.** That gap between long-term intent and near-term delivery is the venture's opening.

## The Cost of Thermal Generation

This is the commercial heart of the case for new baseload generation.

Per the Auditor General's report on state finances (FY2021/22), as reported:

| Source | Cost per kWh |
|---|---|
| **Thermal (diesel)** | **Rwf 375.5** |
| Hakan peat | Rwf 259.9 |
| Methane | Rwf 173.7 |
| Hydro | Rwf 136.9 |

Two things stand out:

1. **Diesel generation costs roughly Rwf 375/kWh — more than double the ~182 RWF/kWh retail tariff** carried in the earlier repository analysis. The difference is met through subsidy. EUCL reported a loss of **Rwf 52.5 billion** that year, with government providing more than **Rwf 39 billion** in tariff subsidy.
2. **Thermal accounted for 31% of total production expenses while supplying only 14% of the energy mix.** That ratio is the fiscal burden that new baseload capacity relieves.

**Confidence note:** these figures are secondary reporting of the Auditor General's report. The article body and the underlying report could not be retrieved directly. **Verify against the AG report itself before using them in any external submission.**

### Thermal capacity today

- **Jabana 1** — 7.8 MW, diesel, commissioned 2004
- **Jabana 2** — 21 MW, HFO/diesel, commissioned 2009
- Sub-total: **28.8 MW installed / 27.4 MW available**

Since 2023, thermal has moved from baseload to **emergency and peak-balancing use only**, because of its cost. It has not been eliminated: the LCPDP still recommends increasing standby thermal to 41 MW and upgrading Jabana 1 from 7.8 MW to 30 MW to maintain a 15% reserve margin, and even models generic new diesel capacity in some 2049–2050 scenarios.

**The implication for this plan:** the opportunity is not to displace diesel as baseload — that has largely already happened. It is to provide firm capacity that reduces the need for *expensive emergency* generation during dry seasons and peak periods, and to reduce the subsidy burden that thermal imposes.

## Competing and Complementary Resources

| Resource | Potential | Current status |
|---|---|---|
| Lake Kivu methane | ~350 MW potential | KivuWatt ~26.4 MW operating |
| Peat | — | Gishoma 15 MW; Hakan project 80 MW |
| Hydro | — | Largest single source; ~35% of generation |
| Solar | — | ~3% of installed capacity; intermittent |
| **Geothermal** | See `GEOTHERMAL_RESOURCE_BASE.md` | **No commercial production** |

Geothermal's distinguishing feature against these alternatives is that it is **baseload and non-emitting**, unlike solar, and does not carry the extraction and environmental questions associated with methane and peat.

## The Policy Position on Geothermal

Two findings matter here, and they point in opposite directions:

**In the long-term plan, geothermal is significant.** The LCPDP 2024 Review reference expansion includes **350 MW of generic geothermal** (an alternative scenario models 150 MW).

**But not soon.** Modelled geothermal additions begin in **2036** (90 MW), then 2037 (60 MW), 2038 (75 MW), 2040 (120 MW), 2049 (5 MW). **Geothermal is not modelled as coming online before the mid-2030s.**

**And the utility's own strategy has no geothermal MW target.** The REG Strategic Plan 2024–2034 lists only "exploratory studies for geothermal resources" as a technical study — no capacity commitment.

**This is the gap the plan must address.** A venture proposing power generation in the late 2020s is ahead of the national plan, which invites the question: why now, and why this? The answer offered by this plan is that the near-term opportunity is **direct heat for industrial and agricultural customers**, which requires no grid interconnection and no new national target, while the power case is prepared in parallel and delivered if and when drilling proves the resource.

## What This Means for the Venture

1. **There is a real fiscal problem to solve** — expensive thermal capacity and a large subsidy — and baseload renewables address it directly.
2. **But the state's own plan does not expect geothermal power before the 2030s.** The venture should not position itself as accelerating a planned power plant; it should position itself as creating commercial heat supply now and building the evidence for power later.
3. **The avoided-cost benchmark for direct use should be the customer's actual fuel, not the retail tariff.** For industrial customers using diesel or HFO, the avoided cost is genuinely high. For those on grid electricity, it is much lower. This distinction is developed in `DIRECT_USE_OPTIONS.md`.
